\section{Alternative Views}
\label{sec:alternative_views}

\paragraph{There is no recovering from retrieval loss.} We argue for applying social choice principles at the aggregation layer of RAG systems, yet our own experiments show how strongly retrieval shapes what that layer receives: aggregation can do little with perspectives retrieval has already discarded. Retrieval already offers a diversity of literature that discuss indexing strategies \citep{wiratunga2024cbr}, knowledge graph construction \citep{edge2024local}, and tree based methods \citep{sarthi2024raptor} among others. Our work does not dispute that literature, but given that our experiments were limited to only show highly relevant passages, we argue that the aggregation layer can still be improved to better handle pluralistic evidence. The two layers compose rather than compete: balanced evidence does not produce balanced answers, and prior work finds models overriding the evidence in front of them \citep{wan-etal-2024-evidence,naphade-2026-rational,wu2024clasheval}. Diverse retrieval is therefore necessary and not sufficient: fixing retrieval alone yields a system that receives every perspective and still reports one. How the two layers combine remains open. Can social choice principles apply at retrieval, so that selection itself preserves proportionality? And does encoding values at several stages compound their effect, or do interventions at retrieval and aggregation cancel?

\paragraph{Interpretive RAG offers limited use.} Another objection concerns the use cases for RAG systems: do we really need interpretive RAG? The answer is that many high-risk domains require more than factual retrieval. Law, medicine, and public policy often involve evidence that is relevant but contested. Existing RAG research has rightly focused on fidelity: ensuring that generated claims are supported by retrieved evidence. In law, \citet{mmagesh2025hallucinationfree} find that leading AI legal research tools continue to produce unsupported statements. In medicine, \citet{nishisako2025reducing} show that domain-specific RAG systems improve groundedness over general-purpose LLMs, but remain imperfect. Fidelity is therefore a necessary benchmark. It is not, however, a sufficient one. Even a system that faithfully retrieves and summarizes its sources may fail if it aggregates competing interpretations into a single conclusion or omits legitimate perspectives. Interpretive RAG systems therefore face a distinct failure mode: not hallucination, but unrepresentative aggregation. As these systems are deployed in high-risk settings, preserving both fidelity and representativeness becomes essential.

\paragraph{Social choice is the wrong formalism.} A second objection targets the framing. A fully social-choice-aware RAG system would be difficult to build. Social choice requires defining who or what counts as a voter, what alternatives are being compared, how duplicated or dependent evidence should be treated, and which normative properties the aggregation rule should satisfy. None of these choices is universal. They depend on the question, the domain, and the purpose of the system. W argue that social choice provides the right language for reasoning about aggregation. It tells us which normative choices exist, which properties are mutually incompatible, and which trade-offs an aggregation procedure makes. Arrow's impossibility theorem \citep{arrow1951socialchoice} reminds us that no aggregation rule satisfies every desirable property at once, while recent work argues that the appropriate rule depends on the application and the values one wishes to preserve \citep{pmlr-v235-conitzer24a}. At the same time, social choice has already proved useful as a framework for learning from diverse human preferences and designing more pluralistic AI systems that can represent diverse values\citep{ovadya2025position}. We make the same claim for interpretive RAG. The goal is not to elect an answer, but to expose the aggregation choices that current systems already make implicitly.
