\section{Alternative Views}
\label{sec:alternative_views}

\paragraph{Retrieval loss is unrecoverable.} We argue for applying social choice principles at the aggregation layer of RAG systems, yet our own experiments show how strongly retrieval shapes what that layer receives: aggregation can do little with perspectives retrieval has already discarded. A substantial literature on indexing strategies already addresses the retrieval layer, as Section~\ref{sec_sub:pluralistic_retrieval} describes. Our experiments show only highly relevant passages, and so remove retrieval unfairness by construction; even then, the aggregation layer handles pluralistic evidence poorly. The two layers compose rather than compete, because balanced evidence does not produce balanced answers. Diverse retrieval is therefore necessary and not sufficient: fixing retrieval alone yields a system that receives every perspective and still reports one. How the two layers combine remains open. Can social choice principles apply at retrieval, so that selection itself preserves proportionality? And does encoding values at several stages compound their effect, or do interventions at retrieval and aggregation cancel?

\paragraph{Interpretive RAG offers limited use.} A second objection concerns the use cases for RAG systems: do we really need interpretive RAG? We do, because many high-risk domains require more than factual retrieval. Law, medicine, and public policy often involve evidence that is relevant but contested. Existing RAG research has rightly focused on fidelity: ensuring that generated claims are supported by retrieved evidence. In law, \citet{mmagesh2025hallucinationfree} find that leading AI legal research tools continue to produce unsupported statements. In medicine, \citet{nishisako2025reducing} show that domain-specific RAG systems improve groundedness over general-purpose LLMs but remain imperfect. Fidelity is therefore a necessary benchmark, not a sufficient one. Even a system that faithfully retrieves and summarises its sources may fail if it aggregates competing interpretations into a single conclusion or omits legitimate perspectives. Interpretive RAG systems therefore face a distinct failure mode: not hallucination, but unrepresentative aggregation. As these systems are deployed in high-risk settings, preserving both fidelity and representativeness becomes essential.

\paragraph{Social choice is the wrong formalism.} A third objection targets the framing. A fully social choice-aware RAG system would be difficult to build. Social choice requires defining who or what counts as a voter, which alternatives are compared, how duplicated or dependent evidence should be treated, and which normative properties the aggregation rule should satisfy. None of these choices is universal; each depends on the question, the domain, and the purpose of the system. We argue nonetheless that social choice provides the right language for reasoning about aggregation. It names the normative choices available, the properties that are mutually incompatible, and the trade-offs an aggregation procedure makes. Arrow's impossibility theorem \citep{arrow1951socialchoice} reminds us that no aggregation rule satisfies every desirable property at once. At the same time, social choice has already proved useful as a framework for learning from diverse human preferences and designing more pluralistic AI systems that can represent diverse values \citep{noothigattu2018voting,freedman2020akidneyallocation,bakker2022finetuning,brill2023proportionality,ovadya2025position}. We make the same claim for interpretive RAG. The goal is not to elect an answer, but to expose the aggregation choices that current systems already make implicitly.
