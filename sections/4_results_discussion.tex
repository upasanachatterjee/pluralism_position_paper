Figure~\ref{fig:expertise_results} shows results for the first experiment, which manipulates expertise signals. Models privilege expertise inconsistently, and the pattern depends on model size. The smaller \texttt{Llama-3.1-8B} model privileges expert bylines for both epistemic and aleatoric questions, more strongly for the epistemic ones, and the effect grows as more passages support each answer. The larger \texttt{Llama-3.3-70B} model shows no significant movement under the byline perturbation.

The academic register reverses direction unexpectedly. Applied to the \emph{yes} passages, it moves neither model significantly towards \emph{yes}; applied to the \emph{no} passages, it moves both models significantly towards \emph{yes}. The expert byline produces the same reversal in the smaller model, which moves significantly towards \emph{yes} when the byline is applied to the \emph{no} passages.

As we discuss in Appendix~\ref{appendix_sub:prompts}, the \emph{yes} passages always appear first, followed by the \emph{no} passages. The lost-in-the-middle effect, compounded by the recency effect, may explain this asymmetry \citep{liu-etal-2024-lost,veseli2025positional,yi-etal-2026-attention}. In the 8B model, many prompts approach the maximum context window (see Appendices~\ref{appendix_sub:models} and~\ref{appendix_sub:prompts} for context windows and prompt sizes), which may be why its effect sizes exceed those of the 70B model. This account does not explain why the register perturbation on the \emph{no} passages moves answers towards \emph{yes}. The uneven distribution of questions across categories (see Appendix~\ref{appendix_sub:dataset}) may confound the result, since the register perturbation may act differently in different categories.

Figure~\ref{fig:matched} restricts the questions to categories that contain both epistemic and aleatoric questions. Only ten questions pass this filter, so we draw no strong conclusions, but the inconsistencies between the two question types persist. Their source may therefore lie elsewhere, in presentation among other factors. Separating these effects requires an evaluation set balanced across categories, with enough questions of each type in each.

\begin{figure}[ht!]
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{images/register_byline_interaction.png}
    \caption{Interaction between register and byline manipulations. The x-axis shows whether compounding helps (positive) or hurts (negative) align the model with the expert perspective.}
    \label{fig:register_byline_interaction}
\end{figure}

The two manipulations also interact. Figure~\ref{fig:register_byline_interaction} shows that they compound inconsistently across models and evidence directions. The smaller \texttt{Llama-3.1-8B} model compounds them for epistemic questions when more passages support the \emph{no} perspective; the larger model does so for the \emph{yes} perspective. For aleatoric questions the interaction appears to reverse, pushing the model away from the expert perspective. Whether that reversal reflects appropriate discounting of expertise on aleatoric questions or another artefact of presentation, our data cannot say.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\begin{figure}
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{images/stability.png}
    \caption{Stability under passage order and selection perturbations. The x-axis shows the percentage of questions for which the model's answer changed under different random seeds or passages.}
    \label{fig:stability}
\end{figure}

Where evidence sits in the prompt strongly affects the model's answer. This is a known limitation of RAG systems \citep{liu-etal-2024-lost,veseli2025positional,yi-etal-2026-attention,naphade-2026-rational}, and it bears directly on the pluralistic groundedness of their answers. We ran two further experiments: one shuffled the order of the passages under five random seeds, the other re-drew the sample of passages for each answer. Figure~\ref{fig:stability} shows that the answers are unstable under both perturbations. The smaller \texttt{Llama-3.1-8B} model is more sensitive to passage selection, the larger \texttt{Llama-3.3-70B} model to passage order. The instability originates in the retrieval layer, but the aggregation layer may yet absorb it: a system could ensemble over passage orders at inference, or route evidence through separate perspective-specific models and combine their outputs \citep{feng-etal-2024-modular}.

This instability clouds the results above, since separating the effects of passage order and selection from those of expertise signals will take further experiments. Even so, the inconsistency of the byline and register manipulations across models and question types suggests that models are \emph{sensitive} to expertise signals without being aligned with human judgements of expertise, and their compounding under larger evidence sets suggests that models grow more sensitive to register as evidence accumulates. Two design directions follow. A system could classify question type before aggregating, routing epistemic and aleatoric questions to different weightings instead of applying one rule to both. Evaluation could then audit that aggregation against social choice properties rather than win rate alone, which rewards agreement with a single passage and says nothing about how several passages combine.

Further work must also establish how expertise interacts with the other features a social choice account would recognise. We examine one such feature across questions in multiple domains. Given an epistemic question about Buddhism, should a practising Buddhist's opinion outweigh that of a Christian who studies Buddhism? Given an aleatoric question about giving children candy on particular days, should a parent's opinion outweigh a non-parent's?
