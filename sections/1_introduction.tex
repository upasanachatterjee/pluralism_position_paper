\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

As language models become more capable, they are increasingly deployed in applications that require reasoning under ambiguity rather than recovering a single correct answer. In many such settings, assuming a single ground-truth label degrades model performance and increases the risk of harm to users \citep{plank-2022-problem,pmlr-v235-sorensen24a}. Models must instead reason pluralistically, recognising that multiple interpretations of the same evidence may be legitimate and producing responses that faithfully reflect that diversity.

Most work on pluralistic reasoning has focused on the \textit{generation} stage. Techniques such as reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) \citep{ouyang2022training} and reinforcement learning from AI feedback (RLAIF) \citep{bai2022constitutionalaiharmlessnessai} encourage models to generate outputs that better reflect diverse values and preferences. Yet many language model applications rely on retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), where generation is constrained by the evidence retrieved beforehand. In these systems, the central challenge is not generation alone but \textit{groundedness}: whether a model's answer faithfully reflects the evidence on which it is based.

Groundedness is commonly understood as fidelity to the retrieved context. We argue that this definition is incomplete. Between retrieval and generation, a model implicitly aggregates evidence by deciding which documents to attend to, which claims to privilege, and how to reconcile conflicting information. A model may faithfully cite its retrieved context while over-weighting authoritative language, ignoring minority perspectives, or collapsing competing explanations into a single narrative. Groundedness therefore depends not only on \emph{what} evidence is retrieved, but also on \emph{how} that evidence is aggregated.

Consider the question highlighted in Figure~\ref{fig:causal_pluralism_social_choice}: ``Does the minimum wage kill jobs?'' A labour organiser may answer ``no,'' while a business owner may answer ``yes.'' Their disagreement is not merely normative. Both may appeal to overlapping empirical evidence while interpreting that evidence through different value systems, priorities, or even different causal models \citep{godfrey-smith2010causal}. A grounded system should therefore preserve these competing interpretations when they are supported by the available evidence rather than collapsing them into a single account.

These perspectives do not reach a retrieval system as tidy, directly statements. They arrive as documents that differ in nearly every respect except their bearing on the question. The example above might yield a blog post by a line cook laid off after their employer's payroll costs rose, a working paper on wage floors and self-reported quality of life, and a testimonial on a housing charity's website where a worker describes finally saving enough to buy a house. Each carries a different kind of authority: lived experience, statistical inference, institutional endorsement. Each also speaks in distinct registers, levels of generality, and topics. All three bear on whether the minimum wage kills jobs.

This heterogeneity makes aggregation difficult. A retriever that scores passages by semantic similarity to the query will rank the working paper highly and the testimonial not at all, since the testimonial shares almost no vocabulary with the question. A model reading the retrieved passages may treat academic phrasing and a credentialed byline as marks of evidential weight, and defer to the working paper. Even the count of documents misleads: two of the three passages here favour one side, but that may reflect availability rather than how the weight is distributed. Current RAG systems resolve these questions implicitly, through similarity scores and stylistic priors, rather than through any stated account of how much weight each source deserves.

Recognising multiple legitimate perspectives, however, does not tell us how they should be represented. A RAG pipeline answers that question twice, and both times implicitly. The retriever selects a small subset of documents from a much larger corpus; the language model combines those documents into a single response. Each stage decides which perspectives survive, which are amplified, and which are discarded. Groundedness is therefore a problem that spans the pipeline rather than a property of retrieval or generation alone.

A rich body of work investigates how to improve representativeness at the document retrieval layer (see
Section~\ref{sec_sub:pluralistic_retrieval}); far less considers how the retrieved evidence should then be aggregated. If two perspectives are represented in the retrieved passages, does the model preserve that balance? Does rhetorical authority outweigh evidential support? Do changes in the amount or distribution of evidence systematically distort the resulting answer? These questions concern the aggregation of evidence rather than its retrieval or phrasing.

We argue that existing RAG systems provide little support for this stronger notion of groundedness. Prior work shows that RAG pipelines exhibit a factual bias that narrows the range of information retrieved and synthesised \citep{agrawal2026retrievalaugmentedgenerationfactualgrounding}, while pluralistic alignment methods intervene almost exclusively at the level of generation. Groundedness should instead be understood as faithful and proportionate evidence aggregation throughout the pipeline. This paper diagnoses that gap rather than closing it. Naming the aggregation choices a pipeline already makes is the first step towards stating them openly. Any framework that would state them, social choice theory \citep{arrow1951socialchoice} among them, must reach the retrieval stage and not generation alone.

The main contributions of this paper are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A survey of current work on pluralistic reasoning and groundedness in retrieval-augmented generation, identifying where existing approaches fall short of faithful aggregation.
    \item An initial investigation of how models aggregate conflicting evidence on two question axes. We perturb evidence size and credentialed versus stylistic cues of expertise. We find that models privilege these cues inconsistently, and that their effects compound in ways that do not align with human judgements.
    \item A discussion of the normative choices these pipelines currently make implicitly, and of why frameworks such as social choice theory can be used to inform the aggregation process at both retrieval and generation.
\end{itemize}
