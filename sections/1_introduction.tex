\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

As language models become more capable, they are increasingly deployed in applications that require reasoning under ambiguity rather than recovering a single correct answer. In many such settings, assuming a single ground-truth label degrades model performance and increases the risk of harm to users \citep{plank-2022-problem,pmlr-v235-sorensen24a}. Models must instead reason pluralistically, recognising that multiple interpretations of the same evidence may be legitimate and producing responses that faithfully reflect that diversity.

Most work on pluralistic reasoning has focused on the \textit{generation} stage. Techniques such as reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) \citep{ouyang2022training} and reinforcement learning from AI feedback (RLAIF) \citep{bai2022constitutionalaiharmlessnessai} encourage models to generate outputs that better reflect diverse values and preferences. Yet many language model applications rely on retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), where generation is constrained by the evidence retrieved beforehand. In these systems, the central challenge is not generation alone but \textit{groundedness}: whether a model's answer faithfully reflects the evidence on which it is based.

Groundedness is commonly understood as fidelity to the retrieved context. We argue that this definition is incomplete. Between retrieval and generation, a model implicitly aggregates evidence by deciding which documents to attend to, which claims to privilege, and how to reconcile conflicting information. A model may faithfully cite its retrieved context while over-weighting authoritative language, ignoring minority perspectives, or collapsing competing explanations into a single narrative. Groundedness therefore depends not only on \emph{what} evidence is retrieved, but also on \emph{how} that evidence is aggregated.

Consider the question highlighted in Figure~\ref{fig:causal_pluralism_social_choice}: ``Does the minimum wage kill jobs?'' A labour organiser may answer ``no,'' while a business owner may answer ``yes.'' Their disagreement is not merely normative. Both may appeal to overlapping empirical evidence while interpreting that evidence through different value systems, priorities, or even different causal models \citep{godfrey-smith2010causal}. A grounded system should therefore preserve these competing interpretations when they are supported by the available evidence rather than collapsing them into a single account.

These perspectives do not reach a retrieval system as tidy, comparable position statements. They arrive as documents that differ in nearly every respect except their bearing on the question. Consider three examples: a blog post by a line cook laid off after their employer's payroll costs rose, a working paper on wage floors and self-reported quality of life, and a testimonial on a housing charity's website where a worker describes finally saving enough to buy a house. Each carries a different kind of authority: lived experience, statistical inference, institutional endorsement. Each also speaks in a register, at a level of generality, and about a subject of its own. All three bear on whether the minimum wage kills jobs.

This heterogeneity makes aggregation difficult. A retriever that scores passages by semantic similarity to the query will rank the working paper highly and the testimonial not at all, since the testimonial shares almost no vocabulary with the question. A model reading the retrieved passages may treat academic phrasing and a credentialed byline as marks of evidential weight, thus deferring to the working paper. Even the count of documents misleads: two of the three passages here happen to favour one side, but that may reflect availability rather than weight distribution. Current RAG systems resolve these questions implicitly, through similarity scores and stylistic priors, rather than through any stated account of how much weight each source deserves.

Recognising multiple legitimate perspectives, however, does not tell us how they should be represented. Social choice theory \citep{arrow1951socialchoice} frames that question: it studies how to aggregate multiple judgements or preferences into a collective decision. Retrieval and generation each perform an analogous aggregation over evidence: the retriever selects a small subset of documents from a much larger corpus, while the language model combines those documents into a single response. Both stages determine which perspectives are preserved, amplified, or discarded. Groundedness is therefore an aggregation problem that spans the entire RAG pipeline rather than a property of generation alone.

This framing suggests a different way to evaluate pluralistic RAG. Rather than asking only whether a model's answer is supported by its retrieved context, we should ask whether the model faithfully aggregates competing evidence once that context has been retrieved. If two perspectives are represented in the retrieved evidence, does the model preserve that balance? Does rhetorical authority outweigh evidential support? Do changes in the amount or distribution of evidence systematically distort the resulting answer? These questions concern the aggregation of evidence rather than its retrieval or phrasing.

We argue that existing RAG systems provide little support for this stronger notion of groundedness. Prior work shows that RAG pipelines exhibit a factual bias that narrows the range of information retrieved and synthesised \citep{agrawal2026retrievalaugmentedgenerationfactualgrounding}, while pluralistic alignment methods intervene almost exclusively at the level of generation. Groundedness should instead be understood as faithful and proportionate evidence aggregation throughout the pipeline. Legitimate perspectives may diverge for many reasons. Whatever their source, social choice theory provides a normative framework for reasoning about how they should be represented and aggregated, and that framework motivates a richer conception of groundedness for retrieval-augmented generation.

The main contributions of this paper are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A survey of current work on pluralistic reasoning and groundedness in retrieval-augmented generation, which identifies the limitations of existing approaches.
    \item An experimental investigation of how models aggregate conflicting evidence on epistemic and aleatoric questions, which shows that language models do not always privilege expertise appropriately.
    \item A discussion of what social choice theory implies for the design of grounded RAG systems, which sets out the normative choices these pipelines currently make implicitly.
\end{itemize}
