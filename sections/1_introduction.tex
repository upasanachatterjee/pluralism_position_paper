\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

As language models become more capable, they are increasingly deployed in applications that require reasoning under ambiguity rather than recovering a single correct answer. In many such settings, assuming a single ground-truth label degrades model performance and increases the risk of harm to users \citep{plank-2022-problem,pmlr-v235-sorensen24a}. Models must instead reason pluralistically: they must recognize that multiple interpretations of the same evidence may be legitimate and produce responses that faithfully reflect that diversity.

Most work on pluralistic reasoning has focused on the \textit{generation} stage. Techniques such as reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) \citep{ouyang2022training} and reinforcement learning from AI feedback (RLAIF) \citep{bai2022constitutionalaiharmlessnessai} encourage models to generate outputs that better reflect diverse values and preferences. Yet many language model applications rely on retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), where generation is constrained by the evidence retrieved beforehand. In these systems, the central challenge is not generation alone but \textit{groundedness}: whether a model's answer faithfully reflects the evidence on which it is based.

Groundedness is commonly understood as fidelity to the retrieved context. We argue that this definition is incomplete. Between retrieval and generation, a model implicitly aggregates evidence by deciding which documents to attend to, which claims to privilege, and how to reconcile conflicting information. A model may faithfully cite its retrieved context while nevertheless over-weighting authoritative language, ignoring minority perspectives, or collapsing competing explanations into a single narrative. Groundedness therefore depends not only on \emph{what} evidence is retrieved, but also on \emph{how} that evidence is aggregated.

Consider the question highlighted in Figure~\ref{fig:causal_pluralism_social_choice}:  ``Does the minimum wage kill jobs?'' A labour organiser may answer `no,'' while a business owner may answer ``yes.'' Their disagreement is not merely normative. Both may appeal to overlapping bodies of empirical evidence while interpreting that evidence through different causal models of the relationship between minimum wages, employment, and the broader economy. They may also differ in how they evaluate the strength and relevance of competing evidence. \citet{godfrey-smith2010causal} describes this phenomenon as \textit{causal pluralism}: the view that there are multiple legitimate ways to understand causal relationships, each appropriate to different domains, purposes, or explanatory contexts. A grounded system should therefore preserve these competing interpretations when they are supported by the available evidence rather than prematurely collapsing them into a single account.

Recognizing multiple legitimate perspectives, however, does not tell us how they should be represented. That question is naturally framed by social choice theory \citep{arrow1951socialchoice}, which studies how multiple judgments or preferences can be aggregated into a collective decision. Retrieval and generation each perform an analogous aggregation over evidence: the retriever selects a small subset of documents from a much larger corpus, while the language model combines those documents into a single response. Both stages determine which perspectives are preserved, amplified, or discarded. Groundedness is therefore an aggregation problem that spans the entire RAG pipeline rather than a property of generation alone.

This perspective suggests a different way to evaluate pluralistic RAG. Rather than asking only whether a model's answer is supported by its retrieved context, we should ask whether the model faithfully aggregates competing evidence once that context has been retrieved. If two perspectives are represented in the retrieved evidence, does the model preserve that balance? Does rhetorical authority outweigh evidential support? Do changes in the amount or distribution of evidence systematically distort the resulting answer? These questions concern the aggregation of evidence rather than its retrieval or verbalization alone.

We argue that existing RAG systems provide little support for this stronger notion of groundedness. Prior work shows that RAG pipelines exhibit a factual bias that narrows the range of information retrieved and synthesized \citep{agrawal2026retrievalaugmentedgenerationfactualgrounding}, while pluralistic alignment methods intervene almost exclusively at the level of generation. We contend that groundedness should instead be understood as faithful and proportionate evidence aggregation throughout the pipeline. Causal pluralism provides the epistemic framework for identifying legitimate perspectives, and social choice theory provides the normative framework for reasoning about how those perspectives should be represented and aggregated. Together, they motivate a richer conception of groundedness for retrieval-augmented generation.
