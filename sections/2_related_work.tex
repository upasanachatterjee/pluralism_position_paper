\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) is commonly evaluated along three
dimensions: context relevance, groundedness, and answer relevance. Groundedness
usually refers to whether a model's output is supported by its retrieved
context. We argue that this definition is incomplete for pluralistic settings.
A grounded answer must not only reflect retrieved evidence; the retrieved
evidence must also represent the relevant perspectives in the underlying
corpus, and the model must combine those perspectives faithfully. We review
three areas of prior work: groundedness evaluation in RAG, retrieval methods
that determine which evidence reaches the model, and pluralistic alignment
methods that study how models represent diverse perspectives.

\subsection{Groundedness in RAG}

Prior work on groundedness asks whether models follow the evidence available in
their context. This literature divides into epistemic and aleatoric settings.
Epistemic settings contain a single correct answer. Aleatoric settings contain
multiple defensible answers or perspectives.

\paragraph{Epistemic Groundedness.}
\citet{ravichander-etal-2022-condaqa} test whether models answer yes-or-no
questions consistently under small changes to retrieved passages. They consider
three edits to negated statements: paraphrase, scope change, and negation
reversal. One passage states that `[n]early all of his possessions were
destroyed with the exception of a guitar and a prized Jaguar automobile.'' A
scope edit changes this to `[n]early all of his possessions were destroyed
(including a guitar), with the exception of a prized Jaguar automobile.'' This
change reverses the answer to \textit{Was Parsons able to use his guitar after
the fire?} from yes to no. The strongest model tested, UnifiedQA-v2-3B, answers
almost three-quarters of individual questions correctly but remains
inconsistent across equivalent versions of the passage. Scope and negation
changes produce the largest failures, with consistency falling to chance or
below. \citet{qi-etal-2024-long2rag} find similar weaknesses in long-context
settings, where performance varies across domains and question types.

\paragraph{Aleatoric Groundedness.}
Aleatoric groundedness concerns cases where multiple answers may be justified.
\citet{wan-etal-2024-evidence} study which evidence models find convincing.
They introduce ConflictingQA, a benchmark that pairs contested questions, such
as \textit{Is aspartame linked to cancer?}, with web passages supporting
opposing answers. The model receives one passage for each stance and is
evaluated by a \textit{win rate}: the proportion of comparisons in which the
model's answer matches a passage's position. Win rate depends strongly on
semantic relevance. Human-relevant signals, such as scientific references and
quantitative evidence, have little effect, while a simple prefix that restates
the query substantially increases a passage's influence. Their setting tests
whether models follow individual pieces of evidence, but it does not test how
models combine multiple pieces of evidence supporting competing positions.
\citet{naphade-2026-rational} show that convincingness changes when several
pieces of evidence support each position.
\citeposs{wu2024clasheval} work further shows that even frontier models often
ignore retrieved evidence that conflicts with their prior beliefs.

Together, these results show that models remain sensitive to changes in
retrieved evidence. However, existing groundedness evaluations treat the
retrieved context as fixed. They ask whether a model follows its evidence, not
whether it aggregates competing evidence faithfully. This distinction is
central to pluralistic groundedness.

\subsection{Diversity and Coverage in Retrieval}

Retrieval research has long recognized that ranking documents by relevance
alone produces redundant results. Maximal marginal relevance (MMR) reduces
redundancy by balancing query relevance against similarity to previously
selected documents \citep{carbonell1998mmr}. Subtopic retrieval instead
optimizes coverage: \citet{zhai2003subtopic} evaluate rankings by the number of
query subtopics they represent rather than by individual document relevance.
\citet{song2011postranking} diversify retrieval over query intents and
select documents that maximize expected usefulness for users.

These methods provide a foundation for pluralistic retrieval but do not solve
the problem of representing competing positions. First, they diversify across
topics and intents rather than viewpoints. Two documents that support opposing
answers to the same question may appear redundant because they concern the same
topic, even though they represent distinct perspectives. Second, they optimize
average utility. A minority perspective contributes less to expected utility
and may therefore be excluded. This conflicts with the goal of proportional
representation.

Retrieval diversity is therefore necessary but insufficient. A retriever may
return balanced evidence while a model still favors one perspective when
producing an answer. Pluralistic groundedness requires both diverse retrieval
and faithful evidence aggregation.

\subsection{Social Choice and Pluralistic Alignment}

Pluralistic systems must address two related questions: which perspectives
should be represented, and how should competing perspectives be aggregated?
Social choice theory provides a formal framework for studying the second
question. It examines how multiple individual preferences or judgments can be
combined into collective decisions. Classical results, such as Arrow's
impossibility theorem \citep{arrow1951socialchoice}, show that no aggregation
rule satisfies all desirable properties in every setting. Social choice
therefore does not prescribe a universal solution to disagreement; instead, it
reveals the trade-offs involved when combining competing perspectives.

These trade-offs depend on the context in which aggregation occurs. The values
that guide a medical allocation problem, such as kidney assignment, differ from
those that guide ethical decisions for autonomous vehicles
\citep{freedman2020akidneyallocation,noothigattu2018voting}. Preferences also
vary across cultural, demographic, and socioeconomic groups. In the autonomous
vehicle dilemma, for example, moral judgments differ substantially across
countries, with collectivist and individualist cultures exhibiting different
preferences over how vehicles should prioritize competing harms
\citep{awad2018moral}. \citet{pmlr-v235-conitzer24a} argue that when preferences
diverge strongly across groups, aggregation methods must account for the
specific context and the values of the communities involved.

These questions have recently emerged in the study of pluralistic alignment.
\citet{pmlr-v235-sorensen24a} identify three targets for pluralism in language
models: Overton models, which present a range of reasonable responses;
steerable models, which adopt a specified perspective; and distributional
models, which reflect the distribution of views in a population. They also find
evidence that standard alignment procedures can reduce distributional
pluralism.

Subsequent work has explored different approaches to representing diverse
perspectives within models. \citet{bakker2022finetuning} fine-tune a
70B-parameter model to generate statements that maximize expected approval
among people with different views, ranking candidate statements with reward
models under several aggregation rules. \citet{feng-etal-2024-modular} route a
black-box LLM through a collection of community models, allowing additional
perspectives to be incorporated without retraining the base model.
\citet{pmlr-v235-conitzer24a} argue that social choice theory provides a
principled framework for aggregating diverse feedback during alignment.

However, these approaches operate primarily on the model rather than the
evidence it receives. They modify training data, reward functions, or inference
procedures, while assuming that the model has access to an adequate
representation of relevant perspectives. RAG introduces an additional
constraint: the model can only reason over the perspectives contained in its
retrieved context. A pluralistic RAG system must therefore address the
aggregation problem before and during generation. Our work studies this missing
step: given a set of retrieved perspectives, can a model aggregate evidence
faithfully under controlled changes to the retrieved context?
