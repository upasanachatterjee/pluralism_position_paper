\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) is commonly evaluated along three dimensions: context relevance, groundedness, and answer relevance. We argue that a grounded answer must not only reflect retrieved evidence; the retrieved evidence must also represent the relevant perspectives in the underlying corpus, and the model must combine those perspectives faithfully. We review three areas of prior work from this perspective: groundedness evaluation in RAG, retrieval methods that determine which evidence reaches the model, and pluralistic alignment methods that study how models represent diverse perspectives.

\subsection{Groundedness in RAG}

Prior work on groundedness asks whether models follow the evidence available in their context. This literature divides into epistemic and aleatoric settings. Epistemic settings contain a single correct answer. Aleatoric settings contain multiple defensible answers or perspectives.

\paragraph{Epistemic Groundedness.}
\citet{ravichander-etal-2022-condaqa} test whether models answer yes-or-no questions consistently under small changes to retrieved passages. They consider three edits to negated statements: paraphrase, scope change, and negation reversal. One passage states that ``[n]early all of his possessions were destroyed with the exception of a guitar and a prized Jaguar automobile.'' A scope edit changes this to ``[n]early all of his possessions were destroyed (including a guitar), with the exception of a prized Jaguar automobile.'' This change reverses the answer to \textit{Was Parsons able to use his guitar after the fire?} from yes to no. The strongest model tested, UnifiedQA-v2-3B, answers almost three-quarters of individual questions correctly but remains inconsistent across equivalent versions of the passage. Scope and negation changes produce the largest failures, with consistency falling to chance or below. \citet{qi-etal-2024-long2rag} find similar weaknesses in long-context settings, where performance varies across domains and question types. This highlights challenges in groundedness even in cases where is there a valid gold label, and low conflict in the evidence. 

\paragraph{Aleatoric Groundedness.}
Aleatoric groundedness concerns cases where multiple answers may be justified. \citet{wan-etal-2024-evidence} study which evidence models find convincing. They introduce ConflictingQA, a benchmark that pairs contested questions, such as ``Is aspartame linked to cancer?'', with web passages supporting opposing answers. The model receives one passage for each stance and is evaluated by a \textit{win rate}: the proportion of comparisons in which the model's answer matches a passage's position. Win rate depends strongly on semantic relevance. Human-relevant signals, such as scientific references and quantitative evidence, have little effect, while a simple prefix that restates the query substantially increases a passage's influence. Their setting tests whether models follow a single piece of evidence, not how models combine several pieces supporting competing positions. \citet{naphade-2026-rational} show that convincingness changes inconsistently once each position is supported by several passages. \citeposs{wu2024clasheval} work shows further that even frontier models often ignore retrieved evidence that conflicts with their prior beliefs.

Together, these results show that models remain sensitive to changes in retrieved evidence. Existing groundedness evaluations, however, treat the retrieved context as fixed. They ask whether a model follows its evidence, not whether it aggregates competing evidence following human values and priorities. This distinction is central to pluralistic groundedness.

\subsection{Diversity and Coverage in Retrieval}
\label{sec_sub:pluralistic_retrieval}

Pluralistic retrieval builds on a long line of work in retrieval diversification. Rather than returning only the highest-scoring documents, diversification methods seek rankings that expose users to multiple facets of a query. Maximal marginal relevance (MMR) reduces redundancy by balancing query relevance against similarity to previously selected documents \citep{carbonell1998mmr}. Subtopic retrieval optimises coverage across different aspects of a query \citep{zhai2003subtopic}, while later work diversifies over query intents to maximise expected usefulness for users \citep{song2011postranking}. Graph based systems are also increasing in popularity as a way to enable more context-aware retrieval than traditional vector-based methods \citep{sarthi2024raptor, edge2024local}.

These methods provide a foundation for pluralistic retrieval but do not solve the problem of representing competing positions. First, they diversify across topics and intents rather than viewpoints. Two documents that support opposing answers to the same question may appear redundant because they concern the same topic, even though they represent distinct perspectives. Second, they optimise average utility. A minority perspective contributes less to expected utility and may therefore be excluded. This conflicts with the goal of proportional representation.

Retrieval diversity is therefore necessary but insufficient. A retriever may return balanced evidence while a model still favors one perspective when producing an answer. 

\subsection{Social Choice and Pluralistic Alignment}

Pluralistic systems must address two related questions: which perspectives to represent, and how to aggregate them. Social choice theory provides a formal framework for studying the second question. It examines how multiple individual preferences or judgements can be combined into collective decisions. Classical results, such as Arrow's impossibility theorem \citep{arrow1951socialchoice}, show that no aggregation rule satisfies all desirable properties in every setting. Social choice therefore does not prescribe a universal solution to disagreement; instead, it reveals the trade-offs involved when combining competing perspectives.

These trade-offs depend on the context in which aggregation occurs. The values that guide a medical allocation problem, such as kidney assignment, differ from those that guide ethical decisions for autonomous vehicles \citep{freedman2020akidneyallocation,noothigattu2018voting}. Preferences also vary across cultural, demographic, and socioeconomic groups. In the autonomous vehicle dilemma, for example, moral judgements differ substantially across countries, with collectivist and individualist cultures exhibiting different preferences over how vehicles should prioritise competing harms \citep{awad2018moral}. \citet{pmlr-v235-conitzer24a} argue that when preferences diverge strongly across groups, aggregation methods must account for the specific context and the values of the communities involved.

These questions have recently emerged in the study of pluralistic alignment. \citet{pmlr-v235-sorensen24a} identify three targets for pluralism in language models: Overton models, which present a range of reasonable responses; steerable models, which adopt a specified perspective; and distributional models, which reflect the distribution of views in a population. They also find evidence that standard alignment procedures can reduce distributional pluralism.

Subsequent work has explored different approaches to representing diverse perspectives within models. \citet{bakker2022finetuning} fine-tune a 70B-parameter model to generate statements that maximise expected approval among people with different views, ranking candidate statements with reward models under several aggregation rules. \citet{feng-etal-2024-modular} route a black-box LLM through a collection of community models, allowing additional perspectives to be incorporated without retraining the base model. \citet{pmlr-v235-conitzer24a} argue that social choice theory provides a principled framework for aggregating diverse feedback during alignment.

These approaches, however, operate primarily on the model rather than on the evidence it receives. They modify training data, reward functions, or inference procedures, while assuming that the model has access to an adequate representation of relevant perspectives. RAG introduces an additional constraint: the model ought to only reason over the perspectives contained in its retrieved context. Our work studies this missing step: given a set of retrieved perspectives, can a model aggregate evidence faithfully under controlled changes to the retrieved context?
